A Different Kind of Singer

2026 · 9 min
Graphite, 2026

Strange. This cloth has holes.

“Dadi,” I say, pulling at my grandmother’s sleeve, “why does this cloth have holes?”

“That’s chikan embroidery, beta.”

“Chicken?”

I look at this piece of cotton fabric in my nine-year-old hands. I rub the material in between my thumb and forefinger. It feels wispy. Soft. Like a feather. Is that why it’s called chicken?

Dadi is cutting more of this material from her large fabric roll. It’s a thick white pillar of cloth, wrapped around a long cardboard tube: about the same height as me – a hundred and twenty centimeters or so.

We’re in her bedroom. It smells like a tailor shop – cut linen, steamed cotton… a faint scent of cedarwood from her storage closet. That’s where she keeps her fabric scissors, needle pincushion, and three hundred feet long measuring tape. Instead of a conventional bedside table, Dadi has a large, cloth-covered wooden cutting table next to her bed, where she keeps more scissors and pencils. But that table is mostly home to the little crafts I made at school. My Humpty Dumpty inspired egg rests on her cutting table like a watchful guardian. Two googly eyes and a marker-drawn tie adorn its white shell. Three dots vertically placed on its center are its coat buttons.

After cutting up the necessary length of chikan cloth, Dadi takes it to the living room, where her 80-year-old treadle sewing machine is: the Singer. I skip along behind her, the fabric rippling in my hand.

Her sewing machine is an antique contraption, so you might be imagining a rusted exterior.

Or maybe fully faded “Singer” letters along its body?

Perhaps a creaky hand wheel?

Well, the way this sewing machine’s metallic black exterior gleams in the sunlight, and the way the rhythmic movement of its needle thrums with energy, you wouldn’t believe this machine is nearly a century old. The beige-colored cloth Dadi uses to wipe the dust off its surface is folded beside it. The rest of the table is neat and uncluttered – as always.

Dadi is going to stitch my barbie doll a new lab coat. A chikan cloth one. When she takes a seat at her chair, I hover next to her, chikan fabric still in my hands.

She puts on her eyeglasses, which hang around her neck, attached to an auburn beaded chain. The beads catch the yellow lamp light at her desk and glimmer like the ocean under the sun. She keeps her dark hair, with some strands of silver peeking through, out of the way using a square cotton cloth which she ties around her head like a bandana. Her eyebrows furrow when she places the cloth on the Singer’s throat plate. Her spectacles rest on the bridge of her nose like a balanced seesaw. An onlooker would mistake her expression for irritation. But I know that it’s simply what she looks like when she’s concentrating.

I twisted the cloth in my hand. “But Dadi,” I said, when the Singer paused its rhythmic clicking. “Why is it called chicken?”

*

In the summer of 2025, I toured the century old Pittock Mansion in Portland, Oregon. It housed several bedrooms, elegantly embroidered curtains, large diamond patterned rugs, and delicate wooden furniture.

But none of those details struck me as strongly as what I saw in the sewing room: a gleaming black Singer sitting on a light wooden desk. A spool of shining white thread adorned its top. Its “Singer” letters were printed in gold – a block, fancy font, different from Dadi’s lower case letters. Its balance wheel shone in the sunlight. It seemed excited to rotate, to rhythmically click with the push of the pedal. Its needle glinted in the light – inviting rather than malicious.

In January of the same year, we’d visited Pakistan to attend my cousin’s wedding. Maybe it was because life there was so fast-paced – always some function to get to or new people to go meet – I’d never paused to touch the Singer in our living room throughout my two and a half weeks there.

Now, all I wanted to do was run my hand over its cool, hard metal surface. But a velvet rope and a “do not touch” sign separated us.

When had this mansion’s machine last run? Did it ever make two textiles one connected piece? Had it neatened the seam of a pair of too-long jeans? Stitched a length of ribbon along the edge of a frock for someone’s newborn granddaughter?

I pulled my phone out of my pocket to snap a photo so I could share it with Dadi later.

*

Sometime near the beginning of my second semester, I called Dadi to ask her how she was doing. Our conversations almost always start something like this:

“Are you going to play cards at Karachi Gymkhana tonight?”

“Yes, my friends have been missing me. They all say, ‘Khatoon, it’s no fun without you!’”

I laughed and said, “I’m sure. And I bet you’re also on the machine?”

“Yes, of course! I have two orders for night suits, and in the mornings I do my crochet.”

“I’m glad you’re always keeping yourself busy.”

“Yes, this is my passion. I can’t do without it!”

We talked a bit more and then I told her I had to get going.

“Ok, beta, keep calling. I love to hear your voice.”

When my parents, brother, and I moved to Dubai end of 2016, Dadi was on her own in the house.

After I started at UBC, I realized one day, after we ended one of our calls, that I’m on my own like her too.

Flying to Canada solo for the first time was one thing. Getting used to a dorm life where laundry only happened when I did it, tomatoes would only get sliced if I cut them, a glass of water would rest on my desk only if I brought myself one…was another. I had to get used to no last load warnings from Mama before she ran the laundry machine. I no longer smelled her seasoned, saffron-infused biryani’s spicy warm fragrance make its way to the living room from the kitchen. I couldn’t ask my brother to bring me a glass of water while I stayed in my cozy spot on the sofa.

So, I can imagine what Dadi would’ve felt the morning after we travelled: waking up to a house where silence was the loudest sound.

But the Singer, her close and loyal friend, kept her busy throughout that transition.

*

Earlier this year in Vancouver, when the rain finally let the sun shine, my friend Keira and I stepped into the Makers shop in Kitsilano, feeling warmth near the window where sunlight seeped through. We picked up on scents of vanilla, cinnamon, and citrus from the scented candle stand at the entrance.

The label on one of them read “Grandma’s Kitchen.” It smelled like the remnants of a buttery homemade apple pie fresh out of the oven.

I noticed they were missing a candle labelled “Grandma’s Sewing”; one that I’d imagine is linen-scented, mingled with a note of cedarwood… and whatever spools of ribbon smell like.

We walked deeper into the store, past greeting cards with watercolor landscapes of Kitsilano beach, Granville Island, and English Bay beach. Then I spotted a dark wooden shelf with floral patterns embroidered on light-wood linen hoops.

I pulled my phone out of my pocket to snap a photo. Then I sent it to Dadi.

I texted her, “hello dadi, I went to a makers shop where there’s handmade items and jewelry and this reminded me of you.”

A few hours later, at her 8:44 am, she replied, “Oh that’s lovely I like all of them”

When I clicked on her message to respond, I happened to scroll up slightly to our conversation before this – one of her messages was a reply to my voice note asking her how she’d been doing:

“Minhal so nice to hear your voice I am very busy I have made 60 pot holders some body ordered and now I will cut baby set pieces thanks to God for all that”

I love her long, unpunctuated, run-on sentences.

Then I scrolled back down to our current conversation and answered, “Me too. Especially the dried flowers.”

“Yes you are right,” she said about two hours later.

For one of my early birthday parties in Karachi, Dadi had made several pink and white checkered baskets as thank-you-for-coming giveaways for my friends. They had wooden scaffolding, and Dadi wrapped their handles in white satin ribbon.

In each of them, Mama put chocolate bars and burger-shaped gummy candies. My childhood best friend and fellow artist Huda still puts her craft supplies in it. Over a decade has passed since I last remember seeing my own checkered basket, but maybe it’s still in my childhood bedroom somewhere – home to loom band bracelets and glitter star stickers.

*

After she’s taken her nap, I ask Dadi to tie my hair in a long claw clip. I love the feeling of her cool hands skimming the back of my neck, gently twisting the strands of my long hair into her one hand, while the other snaps the clip near the base of my skull. Click.

Then we go to the living room where she drinks her late afternoon chai. From the white container on the dining table, she takes out vanilla Gluco biscuits and dips them in her cup until they get soft enough to melt in your mouth. I dip my biscuits in her chai too.

In my bedroom, my barbie doll is wearing her new chikan lab coat. Dadi will make another one for her too – a blue floral patterned one this time. From my seat at the dining table, I see that the piece of cloth she’ll use to stitch my barbie doll’s coat is placed next to the Singer already.

But she’ll get to that tomorrow, because later tonight, Dadi is going to play cards with her friends at Karachi Gymkhana.

She’s picked out a blood-orange shalwar kameez for the occasion, finished with white lace along the hem and silver buttons down the front. It sits on her bed, warm after ironing. She asks me to choose her bangles.

I need both hands to carefully carry out the rack of sparkling bangles she keeps at the base of her cedarwood closet. They sway and clink like wind chimes as I bring them to her cutting table. I hop onto the chair beside it, so I don’t have to stand on my toes to figure out which bangle goes with which.

While Dadi applies her mauve shade lipstick at her dressing table and dots her wrists with jasmine-scented perfume, I slip on a couple of her glitter-coated green bangles. Then a few blue ones. And some pink ones after that, until my entire forearm dazzles in the yellow ceiling light. I tilt my arm and they slide off together, tinkling onto the table – too big for my small wrist.

I help Dadi slip on the orange and silver bangles I picked out for her. Her hands are cool to touch. Her skin is soft, like chikan cloth. It stretches a little when she wears the bangles. Then it slowly sinks back in place, like fabric folding over in slow motion.

*

After spending that afternoon at the maker’s shop in Kitsilano, I wondered what Dadi’s display at one might look like. How her pink, ribbon-wrapped handle baskets would be showcased next to the little headcaps she’d stitch for babies, which would sit by a pile of 60 pot holders. Maybe little dresses for barbie dolls made out of chikan cloth would be there too.

Her work would be displayed on a cedarwood shelf. Pink ribbon would adorn its sides. I’d make a gleaming oil painting of her Singer to place on top of the shelf. And underneath that painting would hang a wooden plate – “Grandma’s Sewing” calligraphed across it.
