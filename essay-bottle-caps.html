A Bag of Bottle Caps

January 2025 · 8 min
Graphite, 2026

He stood still as I wrapped him up – neck to ankles – in Mama's long scarf. My little six-year-old brother, now a vibrant navy burrito, stared at me with mischief in his eyes and hands turned to the side like a baby penguin's flippers. His tiny milk teeth sparkled as he giggled, sounds of delight escaping from him in squeaky bursts. I picked him up in both my spindly arms (strong for a petite nine-year-old girl) and spun him around until we could both laugh no more.

My brother was unique in that way; most kids would bolt the other direction if approached by someone wanting to wrap them up with a scarf. Or they'd demand to wrap you in the scarf instead. Very few would cooperate as willingly as my brother did. He followed my instructions exactly and immediately, as though I could do him no wrong. Sometimes, instead of telling him herself, Mama would ask me to tell Mustafa it was time for bed because she knew he'd listen to me more readily. "He'll do anything for his big sister," she had told me. I will always be grateful to her and Baba for forging our bond since we were little; it is titanium: strong, nigh unbreakable.

When I lowered my burrito – uh, brother – back to the ground, we were both breathless. I gripped one end of the scarf and instructed him to spin outward to unwrap himself from his burrito form. He twirled around like a mini tornado, spinning more rapidly with each turn. When his limbs were finally free, he leaped in exhilaration and started wiggling side to side like a Jell-o noodle until we were both sprawled on the carpet, clutching our stomachs and battling the forceful hands of laughter as it attacked us once again.

A couple of years before our burrito escapade, my brother had mistaken the white wall in our house for a large canvas. He must have thought it needed a splash of colour, so unleashed his crayons all over it. He was only four at the time. When our parents found him in the midst of creating his masterpiece, they told him to stop immediately, and brought him to our shared room. The four of us stood in silence until Baba's voice cut the tension: "You know what you did, Mustafa. Please don't do it again."

Did Mustafa know what he had done? He seemed so small next to our giant bookshelf, his hands stuck to his sides and head hanging low, as though the floor had all the answers he ever needed. I remember wanting to squeeze him, he looked so cute – a little repentant doll. When he didn't respond, Mama explained to him that crayons were only to be used on paper, not on everything that was blank and white. He nodded in understanding. Then, still staring at the floor, he walked straight to me and gave me a tight hug, head buried into me and eyes closed firmly. Mama and Baba both started smiling, and I assumed all was forgiven.

One day, Mustafa and I had made our way to the living room to watch The Lion King. I jumped onto the sofa, reclaiming my corner spot, and my brother leaped onto our green Ikea chair on the other side of the room, little legs stretched out on the leg rest in front of him. His feet moved rapidly from side to side, unspent energy surging in his little body. Even as a nine-year-old, I was very tech-savvy, so I had the movie on minutes after we settled in. First we watched the brilliantly orange Savannah sunrise, then the Lion King – Mufasa – came on screen, and I suddenly felt the urge to draw him on my iPad. I paused the movie and told my brother what I planned to do. "Sorry, Mustafa. I'll take a while."

Though I knew he would allow me to pursue my artistic urge, I didn't expect him to continue to sit on the chair, keeping me company because he didn't want me to be alone. Looking back, I realise now how impressive it was that a six-year-old not only exercised the mature trait of patience but also merged it with a poignant sense of consideration. I must have put my fellow nine-year-old kids to shame, surrendering to the selfish urge to draw from the TV screen without a second thought, while a compassionate six-year-old waited till I had completed my drawing.

When Mustafa turned eight and I was eleven, Mama and Baba said that we'd be moving from Karachi to Dubai. That was the first time both my brother and I would be old enough to register and remember a move as significant as this. For as long as we could recall, we were in the same house, in the same school, had the same friends, year after year after year. I remember being so jittery at the thought – somewhat excited, and maybe a little scared – of having to start school somewhere new for the first time. I still remember goosebumps lining my arms the morning of our first day at school in Dubai. I was too nervous to eat breakfast, but Mustafa ate his cereal like it was just another day. Watching him so at ease helped me relax too.

Our new school was only a six minute walk away from home, but it was under the scorching desert sun – one that eagerly drinks up all your energy and leaves you with nothing but sweat-drenched clothes and a burning desire for an air conditioned room and a glass of ice cold water. Despite the terrible heat, Mama came along with us every day to make sure we knew where we were headed. But a couple of weeks into our routine, I had a good enough understanding of the way to and back from school and was ready to relieve Mama of her pick up and drop off duty.

So then, every morning, Mustafa and I would walk to school together and stop in front of the canteen before heading to our classes. That's where I would tell him: "Today, we'll meet right here, okay Mustafa? Don't take too long. And Mama said not to forget to wear your hat." It was Mustafa's grey "Gamer" hat that I used to my advantage when I needed to identify him in the ocean of kids. Sometimes, he'd beat me to our spot and therefore win the chance to tell me about his day first on the walk back home. I won this time though: "We're making a turtle out of bottle caps," I said. "Why?" "For my Craft class. We need to use sustainable items." "We have some bottle caps at home." "Not sure they'll be enough, but I have time to collect more."

Over the course of the week, I couldn't collect as many bottle caps as I wanted, but I think I did a good enough job. I was thinking about where I might acquire more as I waited for Mustafa at our meet up spot, right outside the canteen again. School ended at three o'clock, and Mustafa was usually here about five minutes after the bell rang. But it had been more than ten minutes, and I still couldn't spot him.

I started tapping my foot in irritation, faster and faster, until my calf locked up and I forced myself to stop. I paced under the shade and fanned myself with my hat, trying to keep my growing frustration at bay. My skin prickled, like I was being placed into a preheated oven. My stomach voiced its complaints in a series of odd growling noises. The home time rush was starting to fade as the school grounds got emptier and emptier. My well of patience was nearly empty too, starting to fill up with worry instead. Then finally, I spotted a grey "Gamer" hat bobbing up and down in the distance, making its way down the staircase. I was hit with a wave of relief, which quickly transformed into one of irritation. Where had he been? What had taken him so long?

He had the same mischievous smile on his face as he did when I'd wrapped him up like a burrito two years ago, only now it was a smile with tiny gaps in it, where the milk teeth had fallen out and the permanent ones were starting to make their appearance. His school bag, a little too big for him, weighed him down as he sped toward me. I rushed to meet him, gearing up to shout at him for his lateness. Then I saw in his hand, a Ziploc bag, filled to the brim with red, blue, and green bottle caps, swinging from right to left like a bag full of gems.
